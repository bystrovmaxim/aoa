# Maxitor concept: terms

This dictionary holds the words the change is built on, in the sense the maintainer gives them. Issues and the specification are written in these words. If they need a word that is not here, it is defined here first.

Each term is one entry: the word in bold, then its meaning. When an entry relies on another term, that term is in bold too.

---

## Terms

**Organization** — a Maxitor account: one or more **enterprises** and the **users** who work with them. A studio that works for three clients has one organization and three enterprises. An organization is not a legal entity and not an enterprise. Organizations are isolated from each other: every enterprise and every user belongs to exactly one organization. An organization may connect **SSO**: whoever signs in through it for the first time becomes a user who is active at once, without an invitation.

**User** — a person who works in Maxitor itself, for example an architect or a product owner. A user belongs to exactly one **organization** and may have **access** to several of its **enterprises**. A user is not a C4 Person: a Person uses the enterprise's digital products (a site foreman, a resident, a dispatcher), while a user works with the map and the landscape in Maxitor.

**Organization administrator** — a **user** of the organization marked as an administrator. An administrator invites, blocks, unblocks and removes users, appoints other administrators, creates **enterprises**, appoints the **owner** of an enterprise that has no active owner, and connects **SSO**. There may be several administrators, and at least one active administrator always exists: the last active administrator cannot be blocked, removed or lose the mark. Other users do not see administration at all. A closed enterprise the administrator has no access to is visible to them only in the list: its name, whether it is open or closed, its owner and who has access. The administrator sees the map and the landscape of such an enterprise only after granting access to themselves, and that grant stays in the catalog history like any change.

**User lifecycle** — the states of a **user** in the organization. A user appears either with an invitation sent or already active — when they first sign in through the organization's SSO. An accepted invitation makes the user active, that is, a real user. An active or invited user can be blocked, and a blocked one can be unblocked. A user can be removed from the organization from any state, and removal is final. A blocked or removed user does not work in Maxitor.

**Enterprise access** — a **user's** right to work with an **enterprise** of their **organization**. An **open enterprise** is available to every user of the organization by default, and nobody needs to be listed. A **closed enterprise** is available only to the users of the organization who were granted access explicitly. There may be any number of them. A user of another organization never gets access. For now access is only "yes or no", without roles. The enterprise's **owner** opens and closes it and grants access to it. If there is no active owner (none was appointed, or the owner is blocked or removed), the **organization administrator** does this. Only then may the administrator appoint a new owner. The administrator cannot replace an active owner: otherwise they would have a back door into any closed enterprise.

**Owner** — the **user** who is accountable for an **enterprise**, a **release slice**, a **business area**, a **stage**, a **user story**, a **digital product** or one of our **containers**. Each of them has zero or one owner. An empty owner is a visible signal that nobody has been made accountable yet. An external container and an external system have no owner: we do not run them. Only a user allowed to work with the enterprise that holds the thing can own it. The owner of an enterprise also manages access to it.

**Enterprise** — all of our digital space that serves one end-to-end business process. It holds all the **digital products** and **containers** that automate this process, including supporting ones such as accounting and HR. An enterprise is defined by the process, not by a legal entity and not by infrastructure. For a construction company it is development. For a housing management company it is managing the housing stock, operating it and billing for it, which together make one process. The point of an enterprise is not an inventory of systems but a goal: to set up and improve this process. A company usually has one enterprise; a holding with unrelated businesses has several. Every digital product and every container belongs to exactly one enterprise and is external to all the others. An enterprise is the frame of the story map and of the C4 landscape: everything in them belongs to one enterprise. Every enterprise belongs to exactly one **organization**. A studio that works for three clients runs three enterprises in one organization.

**Enterprise boundary** — the line that separates an **enterprise** from everything else. Our digital products and our containers are inside it. **External systems** and their containers stand on it. The term comes from C4, where this line on the System Landscape diagram separates the enterprise's systems from external ones. Maxitor shows external systems on the boundary but does not run them as its own.

**Software System** — the top C4 level inside an **enterprise**: something that makes sense on its own, without looking inside. It is a generalization. What all of them share (name, description, enterprise) lives in it, and each one has exactly one kind — a specialization: a **digital product** or an **external system**. The word itself is vague, so a particular Software System is named by its kind. The general word is needed where the kinds stand side by side, for example on the System Landscape diagram. Every Software System belongs to exactly one enterprise. A Software System is a logical label: it has no code and makes no calls of its own. So it has no links to other Software Systems of its own either; they are derived from **container links**. For the same reason the list of Software Systems and which containers each one holds are entered by hand: there is nowhere else for them to come from.

**Digital product** — a kind of **Software System**: something that gives value to the people who use it by improving and developing a business process of the **enterprise**. It is not a service. A single **container** rarely carries the whole value; a set of containers with the supporting ones does, so a digital product consists of one or more containers. The reverse is true too: one container may work for several digital products. For example, the residents' mobile app works for both the FSM product and billing. So a digital product does not contain its containers; it gathers them, and the containers belong to the enterprise.

**External system** — a kind of **Software System**: anything we integrate with that lies beyond the **enterprise boundary**, for example the systems of partners, banks and the state. An external system is needed at C1 to show what the enterprise borders on. We do not model its insides, but it has exactly one **container** — its entry point. At C2 and below that container takes part instead of the system: our containers use its **interfaces**. So every real link goes from container to container, with no exception for external systems. An external system is recorded in the enterprise on whose boundary it stands. If the same external system stands on the boundary of two enterprises, each records it separately, because enterprises are isolated from each other.

**Container** — the C4 level below a **Software System**: something that actually runs, for example a service, a database, a mobile app, a web app or a queue. Most often it is a service, so people often call containers services. Every container is recorded in exactly one **enterprise** and is one of two sorts. **Our container** lies inside the **enterprise boundary** and works for zero or more **digital products**. An **external container** is the entry point of exactly one **external system**; it stands on the boundary and belongs to no digital product. A container publishes its **interfaces** (there may be many) and uses those of others (many to many). If our container works for no digital product, that is a signal: it serves no value. A container is the highest level with real links, because only a running part can call anything. A container is created by hand and gets its link right away. Our container is linked to its repository on GitHub — to its **graph history** — and, if the service runs, also to its **live graph**. Through these links Maxitor opens the container's graph and derives its interfaces and integrations from the code, so the landscape is tied to the code. An external container is linked to a web address of the service itself, for example its website or API documentation: we have no source code, so its interfaces and what it uses are entered by hand.

**Component (C3)** — the C4 level inside a **container**: how it is built. For an AOA service these are its domains, actions, entities and adapters. Maxitor's model does not store this level: it is read from the container's graph through the links to the **live graph** and the **graph history**. Below the container only its code knows the truth, so a copy in the catalog would drift away from it.

**Interface** — a set of related endpoints of one **container** on one protocol, for example an admin, mobile or payment interface. The same set of endpoints on another protocol (HTTP and MCP) is another interface. In an AOA service one interface is one adapter. An interface is published by exactly one container, and any number of containers may use it. The interfaces of our container are derived from its code; those of an external container are entered by hand. An interface nobody uses is a visible signal: either it is time to remove it, or someone forgot to use it.

**Container link** — a real link between things that run: **container** A uses an **interface** that container B publishes. So links between containers are many to many, and each link shows its protocol and its meaning — which interface it goes through. An integration with an **external system** is the same kind of link: our container uses an interface of its container. Only such links are recorded. The links of our container are derived from its code: a resource that calls another container declares its target. The links of an external container, for example our interface it sends webhooks to, are entered by hand. For now nothing checks the links; reconciling them with tracing is the next step. A link between two **Software Systems** is not declared but derived: digital product A is linked to product B if some container of A uses an interface of some container of B. A digital product is linked to an external system if one of its containers uses an interface of that system's container. So a link between Software Systems cannot drift away from what really happens between containers. If a container is shared by several products, its link counts for each of them. So a derived link says the products can exchange data, but not that they do so for this particular product.

**User story map** — the development map of the whole **enterprise**. Every enterprise has exactly one map, and every map belongs to exactly one enterprise. The **backbone** runs across the map from left to right, **user stories** lie under it, and **release slices** cut the map from top to bottom.

**Release slice** — a horizontal slice of the **user story map** below the backbone: which **user stories** under all the **stages** make up one portion of value, for example an MVP or "Release 2". Slices are ordered from top to bottom. A slice has a goal (what people will get), an optional target date and an owner. A story lies in zero or one slice. Without a slice it is not planned yet, and an unfinished story moves to the next slice. A release slice is not a code version: one slice holds work in many **containers**, each with its own versions. A slice has no lifecycle of its own yet.

**Initiative** — the answer to "why" for a group of **user stories** of an **enterprise**. An initiative has a kind; for now there is one, PR/FAQ, but other formats of justification may appear. An initiative has zero or more stories, and a story has zero or one initiative. A story without an initiative is a visible signal: for example, an idea before its PR/FAQ, or a technical story. An initiative has exactly one author — the **user** who created it. The author never changes. The state of an initiative follows a simplified lifecycle: draft → approved → done, and it can be rejected from draft and from approved. For now an initiative has only a name; its content will be a set of nodes and will be designed separately.

**Backbone** — the spine of the **user story map**, made of two levels. The upper level is made of **business areas**, the lower one of the **stages** inside them. The backbone reads from left to right as the story of the enterprise's end-to-end process. A business area's card stands over its first stage, as on a paper map, and the following stages go without a card above them.

**Business area** — the upper level of the **backbone**, a large part of the **enterprise's** end-to-end process. For construction these are the land bank, design, construction, sales, finance and operations. For housing management they are acquiring the housing stock, adapting it after purchase, management, operations, routine repairs, cleaning and billing. A business area may be a stage of the process or a cross-cutting function such as finance. A business area is not the same as an AOA domain. A domain is an area inside the code of one application, while a business area is a part of the business on the enterprise's map. They may overlap in meaning, but neither follows from the other. Every business area belongs to exactly one map and has its own place on it in order.

**Stage** — the lower level of the **backbone**, a step inside a **business area**. Every stage belongs to exactly one business area and has its own place in it in order.

**User story** — one story on the **user story map**: who wants to do what and why. Every story lies under one **stage**. It can be moved under another stage and stays the same story. Under one stage stories are ordered by priority from top to bottom. A story points to zero or one **Software System** — where its requirement arises. That may be a digital product or an external system: a requirement may concern an integration with it. It is empty only before **mob elaboration**. On mob elaboration the story is split into **plan steps**.

**Plan step** — one piece of work from a **user story's** plan, done in exactly one **container**. A story may have many steps, and they are ordered. A step's container does not have to belong to the story's Software System: a story of one product may need work in a container of another. This way the story's cross-product impact is visible right on the map. A step on an external container is not our work but a request to the other side. A step's readiness follows its **lifecycle**: for now it has two states, "not done" and "done". A new step is not done. If intermediate states are needed, they are added to the lifecycle. Done steps are a condition for the story to become "done", not a replacement for its state. Two levels of linking are needed because neither follows from the other. The containers do not tell which Software System the story is for: the residents' mobile app is shared by FSM and billing. And the Software System does not tell which containers the story changes.

**User story lifecycle** — the states a **user story** goes through and the allowed moves between them: idea → in elaboration → ready → in progress → done. A story can be rejected from any unfinished state, and from elaboration onwards it can go back if the plan had to be revised. "Ready" means the story has a Software System and a plan of steps. "Done" means all its steps are done and the story is accepted.

**Two times** — Maxitor has two independent time axes. **Catalog time** is the changes of enterprises, maps, initiatives, Software Systems, containers and their links; every change saves the state of the whole catalog, and the track of this time stands in the header on every layer. **Code time** is the versions of a container's graph from its repository and Live; it is today's Maxitor version track, and it stands on the container layer next to the graph. Both times are visible on the container layer because they answer different questions: how the container's code changed, and how its place in the catalog changed (owner, interfaces, products). The axes are not tied automatically: at a past catalog moment the graph does not switch by itself; Maxitor suggests the version that was in effect at that moment.

**Mob elaboration** — working out a user story together: the team, together with the model, clarifies the story, links it to a Software System and splits it into plan steps by container. The **model** here is AI. It reads the story and the containers' graphs and proposes a Software System and a draft of steps: what to change in which container. The team accepts, edits or rejects each proposal. Only what is accepted enters the catalog, and the author of the change is whoever accepted it.

**Catalog editing** — everything people change in the catalog: the map, stories, steps, initiatives, Software Systems, containers, owners. Editing happens in place: a card is dragged across the map, text is edited where it is written, and "+" stands where things are added. Every action is saved at once as a **catalog change** with its author and time, is undone with ⌘Z and is visible on the time track. A past snapshot is read only. Anyone with **enterprise access** may edit: the owner is accountable, not the only one who edits. Editing is live and shared: in a room everyone sees the others' edits at once, and each object shows who is editing it now.

**Deletion** — removing a record from the catalog. Only what nothing refers to can be deleted: a container with no plan steps and no users of its interfaces, a Software System with no stories, empty stages, business areas and release slices. Stories and initiatives are not deleted but rejected: "Rejected" is a state of their lifecycle. What was deleted stays in the catalog history, like any change.

**Catalog comparison** — two moments on the catalog time track: **A** earlier, **B** later, "now" by default. The difference is shown the same way Maxitor compares graph versions: the open view draws the union of A and B. Added is green, removed is red with a dashed outline, changed is amber, the rest is faded. A moved story is amber in its new place, and a faint dashed outline stands in the old one. Clicking something changed shows "before → after" for every property, with its author and time. Records are matched by id, as in the graph comparison, so a rename or a move is a change, not a removal plus an addition. The comparison acts across layers, like a past snapshot. On the container layer Maxitor offers to compare the graph versions that were in effect at A and B as well, but does not switch the graph itself. A comparison is read only: getting the old back is an ordinary edit of the current catalog.

## Not yet defined

- **Initiative content**: not a document but a set of nodes — to be designed separately.
- **Initiative owner**: whether one is needed apart from the author.
- **Release slice lifecycle**: planned, in progress, released, cancelled — to be added later.
- **Rented SaaS we configure ourselves** (for example, a CRM): our container or an external system. Proposal: ours is what we build and run ourselves. Then our configuration is a container, and the vendor's platform is an external system.
- **How a resource declares its target in code**: by the container's key and the interface's name, or by a link to a specific action of the target. The links of our container are derived from this declaration.
- **Our container without AOA** (a front end, a mobile app, a script): where its interfaces and integrations come from.
- **Our container without its own repository** (a database, a queue): what its link is and where its interface comes from; perhaps whoever uses the interface defines it.
- **Checking links**: for now nothing checks the links. The next step is reconciling them with tracing: declared but not called, and called but not declared.
- **Endpoint**: what an interface is made of. For an AOA service these are probably the actions the adapter exposes.
- **A container's private history**: how Maxitor gets access to the history in a private repository if the model must not store the key.
- **Catalog change and the time track**: every catalog change (with its author and time) saves the state of the whole catalog. The time track is a chart of the volume of such changes with a stretchable precision; what counts as a change depends on the open page. On the container layer its own time — the graph versions — stays beside it (see "Two times"). Two moments are compared by the rules of the term "Catalog comparison".
- **Room**: Maxitor already has it (spec 028); now it belongs to the organization and works on every level. Whether it is recorded as an entity of the model or stays the state of the room server is to be decided separately.
- **The user's last place**: where the user stopped (layer, object, tab), so that Maxitor opens there. This is the state of the interface, not the catalog; where to keep it is to be decided separately.
- **Deployment (C4 Deployment)**: where and how containers run — environments (prod, stage), clusters, clouds. It is not the fourth C4 level (that is the source code) but a separate view across the levels: one container may be deployed in several places.
- **Interfaces in the graph**: today there are no AOA adapters in the graph. For our container's interfaces to be derived from code, an adapter must print an interface node into the graph (see `catalog-filling.md`).
- **Person**: who uses the digital products. It is the "who" of a user story. Not to be confused with a Maxitor **user**.
- **Deleting an enterprise**: whether a whole enterprise can be deleted, and who does it.
- **SSO settings**: which providers are supported and how many email domains an organization may have.
- **Handing over an enterprise**: whether an active owner can pass the enterprise to another user.
- **How an organization is created**: who creates it and how its first administrator appears.
- **A user's rights inside an enterprise**: for now access is only "yes or no". If roles are needed (read, edit), the explicit access link gets data of its own and becomes a separate "access grant" entity. Then it will also be decided how a role is set in an open enterprise.
- **Plan step owner**: whether one is needed, or the container's owner is accountable for the step.
- **Plan step and the graph**: a step changes its container's graph. How a step's plan is recorded as an edit of that graph will be decided together with the "graph as a plan" idea.
